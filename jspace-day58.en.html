Jacobian LensJ-lens

Readout toolLinearization
One-line analogy

An intermediate-layer activation is like a table in an old schema version; the final layer is the new schema. The logit lens assumes the column names match and just runs SELECT; the J-lens first derives an average migration script, moves the old table into the new schema, then looks it up in the model's own "dictionary" (the unembedding).

What problem it solves + how it works

Every Transformer layer reads from and writes to the same residual stream (the vector passed between layers — think of it as a shared state bus). To see what layer l is "thinking", the naive approach is the logit lens (nostalgebraist 2020): multiply the intermediate activation directly by the final vocabulary matrix and see which words score highest. The problem: early and middle layers don't share the final layer's coordinate system, so the readout is often commas and spaces. The tuned lens (Belrose et al. 2023) trains an affine translator per layer to match the final output — but what it learns is "what looks most like the next token", so the translator itself may read out things the model doesn't actually use.

Anthropic's July 2026 paper Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models asks a different question: not "what word does this resemble?" but "if I nudge this, which words become more likely for the model to say later?"

lens_l(h) = unembed( J_l · h ),   J_l = E[ ∂h_final / ∂h_l ]
  • h: the activation at some position in layer l; h_final: the final-layer activation;
  • ∂h_final/∂h_l: the Jacobian (a sensitivity table: "if each input dimension wiggles, how much does each output dimension move");
  • E[·]: averaged over lots of ordinary text, all source positions, and the current and all future target positions — so it captures "will say now or later", not just the next token;
  • unembed: the model's own output layer, turning a final-layer vector into vocabulary scores.

Intuition: the real network is nonlinear, but the averaged Jacobian is its first-order linear approximation — an "average influence map" from each layer to the output. It is derived from the model's own derivatives rather than fitted to a decoding objective, so it is less prone to "speaking for the model". The official companion code notes the lenses were fit on about 1000 sequences of 128 tokens of generic web text, and about 100 is already usable.

layer-l activation h──direct──▶unembed▶ logit lens (early layers often read noise)
layer-l activation h──▶average Jacobian J_l──▶unembed▶ J-lens: "what it disposes the model to say"
Code example
import torch
from transformers import GPT2LMHeadModel, GPT2Tokenizer
tok = GPT2Tokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2"); m = GPT2LMHeadModel.from_pretrained("gpt2").eval()
L = 6  # inspect layer 6
def tail(h):  # run from layer L to the end (incl. final LayerNorm)
    for blk in m.transformer.h[L:]:
        out = blk(h); h = out[0] if isinstance(out, tuple) else out
    return m.transformer.ln_f(h)
def hidden(text):
    ids = tok(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    return m(ids, output_hidden_states=True).hidden_states[L].detach()
J, corpus = torch.zeros(768, 768), ["The weather today is", "In 1905 Einstein", "My favorite food is"]
for text in corpus:  # toy: last position → last position only; the real lens also sums future positions
    hs = hidden(text)
    def f(v): h = hs.clone(); h[0, -1] = v; return tail(h)[0, -1]
    J += torch.autograd.functional.jacobian(f, hs[0, -1], vectorize=True) / len(corpus)
h = hidden("The capital of France is")[0, -1]
with torch.no_grad():
    for name, v in [("logit", m.transformer.ln_f(h)), ("J", J @ h)]:
        print(name, [tok.decode(i) for i in m.lm_head(v).topk(5).indices])
Common misconception + practical scenario
Misconception: "The J-lens reads the model's true thoughts." It reads output dispositions under an averaged linear approximation, which can distort in contexts far from the training distribution. And a community replication on GPT-2 small/medium found the J-lens did not beat the logit lens, noting that rank metrics can be gamed by high-frequency tokens. It is more useful on large models; on small ones, not necessarily.
📌 BigCat scenario: when reading any "mind-reading" interpretability news, ask three things first — was the readout fitted or derived? Is the evidence correlational or a causal intervention? At what model scale does it hold? This habit outlasts any single result.
Takeaway + reflection
💡 The logit lens assumes aligned coordinates, the tuned lens learns a translator, the J-lens translates with the model's own derivatives — three trade-offs on the "readout axis".
🤔 The "average Jacobian" compresses thousands of contexts into one matrix. Which kinds of concepts are most likely to be washed out by the averaging?

J-space: A Low-Dimensional Verbalizable SubspaceJ-space

Representation geometryCapacity bottleneck
One-line analogy

The residual stream is a server's entire memory; J-space is the handful of topics on a shared message bus. Most data is each module's local state; only a small part is published to the bus, where any downstream consumer — including the module that "says it out loud" — can subscribe.

What problem it solves + how it works

With the J-lens, you can find, for every word in the vocabulary, the direction that "pushes the model to say it later". The space spanned by these directions is called J-space. The paper reports three geometric findings:

  • Concentrated in middle layers: early layers mostly hold surface features, late layers are busy formatting the next token; the middle layers are the concept-level "workbench";
  • Small share of variance: J-space accounts for less than about 10% of total activation variance — most of the "energy" is spent elsewhere;
  • Limited capacity: it holds roughly k≈25 distinguishable concepts at once — a small register file, not a big memory.

The key point: low variance ≠ unimportant. Within a concept's representation, it is precisely that small J-space component that determines whether it can be reported. This challenges a common default — "the high-variance directions carry the main information" (the PCA intuition). Here, behavior is driven by an inconspicuous low-variance subspace.

Residual stream at a middle layer (schematic)
Non-J component: ≈90%+ variance · syntax/position/surface detailJ-space
↑ small, but both "can be said" and "can be used by reasoning" go through it
Code example
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d, k, n = 512, 25, 2000                        # dims / verbalizable rank / samples
Q = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(d, d)))[0]   # random orthonormal basis
J_dirs, other = Q[:, :k], Q[:, k:]              # first k dims play the role of J-space
# simulated activations: small J component, larger elsewhere (surface, position noise)
acts = rng.normal(size=(n, k)) * 1.0 @ J_dirs.T + rng.normal(size=(n, d - k)) * 1.3 @ other.T
var_J = ((acts @ J_dirs) ** 2).sum() / (acts ** 2).sum()
print(f"J-space variance share: {var_J:.1%}")   # ~3%: PCA would drop it first
# if "saying it" reads only the J component, variance-based reduction deletes the key signal
top_pc = np.linalg.svd(acts - acts.mean(0), full_matrices=False)[2][:50]
print("J energy kept in top 50 PCs:", round(float(((top_pc @ J_dirs) ** 2).sum() / k), 3))
Common misconception + practical scenario
Misconception: "J-space is the model's complete set of thoughts." It is only the verbalizable part; much of the information actually used in computation lives outside it — the model can't say it and the J-lens can't read it. Treat it as a complete log and you get a false sense of security that "not read = never thought".
📌 BigCat scenario: this sits interestingly next to human working-memory research ("about 4±1 chunks") — a capacity bottleneck may be not a bug but the price of making information globally shareable. When designing your own AI workflows, it's worth asking which state must go on the "shared bus" and which can stay local.
Takeaway + reflection
💡 J-space: middle layers, low variance, small capacity — yet it is the gate of reportability.
🤔 If a concept's J component is weakened while the rest stays intact, could the model still "use" it but not "say" it? What human phenomenon does that resemble?

What Can Be Said Is What Silent Reasoning UsesVerbalizable = Used

Causal interventionCore claim
One-line analogy

Audit log vs. primary table: in many systems the "report" is a separately written log that can drift from the real business data. The paper argues J-space is more like a report that reads the primary table directly — when the model reports "what I'm thinking", it reads the very rows its reasoning is using.

What problem it solves + how it works

Day 55 showed that chain-of-thought (CoT) can be unfaithful: the written reasons are not necessarily the real ones. What about reasoning that is never written? The paper uses causal interventions — change one thing, see whether behavior changes — to argue three points:

  • Report: ask the model "what are you thinking about?" and the answer comes from J-space; swap the representation of "soccer" there for "rugby" and the model says it's thinking about rugby — the report is driven by this representation, not merely correlated with it;
  • Directed modulation: tell the model to copy an unrelated sentence while "thinking about citrus fruits", and orange and lemon appear in J-space; ask it to do arithmetic silently, and intermediate results surface in J-space in sequence — without a word spoken;
  • Silent reasoning: in a multi-hop question (e.g. "what color is Mars?"), "Mars" and "color" light up in the middle layers before the model outputs red. Swapping the J component of an intermediate with another problem's flips the answer noticeably more often than swapping the far higher-variance non-J component.

Taken together, the claim is: the reportable representations and the representations used in internal reasoning are the same ones. The other side matters just as much — selectivity: automatic tasks like fluent continuation barely rely on J-space, while flexible reasoning and "reporting experience" do. That is what a "global workspace" should look like: not all computation goes over the bus, only what multiple modules need to use together.

Code example
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
d, k = 64, 8
Q = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(d, d)))[0]; J, R = Q[:, :k], Q[:, k:]
w = J @ rng.normal(size=k)                 # the downstream "answer" reads only the J subspace
def make(c):                                # c = concept vector; non-J part has larger variance
    return J @ c + R @ rng.normal(size=d - k) * 3
def swap(a, b, P):                         # replace a's component in subspace P with b's
    return a - P @ (P.T @ a) + P @ (P.T @ b)
flips_J = flips_R = 0
for _ in range(1000):
    a, b = make(rng.normal(size=k)), make(rng.normal(size=k))
    if np.sign(w @ a) == np.sign(w @ b): continue    # only pairs whose answers differ
    flips_J += np.sign(w @ swap(a, b, J)) == np.sign(w @ b)
    flips_R += np.sign(w @ swap(a, b, R)) == np.sign(w @ b)
print("flips via J swap:", flips_J, " flips via non-J swap:", flips_R)  # the latter is 0 in this toy
Common misconception + practical scenario
Misconception: "If J-space can read silent reasoning, CoT monitoring is obsolete." The J-lens needs white-box weights and reads fuzzy dispositions over the vocabulary, not a full argument; CoT is available black-box and human-readable. They are complementary channels — one shows what was said, the other what is about to be said — and cross-checking them is how you catch "saying one thing, thinking another".
📌 BigCat scenario: in decision support, when a model gives a conclusion but can't explain it well, don't just keep asking "why?" — its verbal explanation may be constructed after the fact. More robust: verify the conclusion itself independently with several different framings, and treat self-reports as leads, not evidence.
Takeaway + reflection
💡 Causal interventions line up "reportable" and "used" on the same representation for the first time — but note this was shown white-box, on controlled tasks.
🤔 If training penalized J-lens readouts (e.g. forbidding certain concepts), would it replay Day 55's "obfuscated reward hacking" — the model moving its intent outside J-space?

Comparison with Global Workspace TheoryGlobal Workspace Theory

Consciousness scienceInterdisciplinaryContested
One-line analogy

Global Workspace Theory sees the brain as many parallel microservices plus a capacity-limited broadcast bus: information that gets onto the bus is subscribed to network-wide and becomes "conscious". Stronger versions also require entry to be all-or-none, like a distributed commit that either takes effect entirely or not at all, rather than gradually.

What problem it solves + how it works

Global Workspace Theory (GWT) was proposed by Bernard Baars in 1988 and later developed into a neural version by Dehaene and others. It aims to explain access consciousness (the kind of "being aware" where information can be reported and used by reasoning): most processing runs unconsciously in parallel, and only a small amount wins the competition, enters the global space, and is broadcast. The neural version also emphasizes ignition: once information crosses a threshold, it spreads abruptly and nonlinearly across the brain.

The paper lists five "workspace-like" properties J-space satisfies:

Verbal report ✓Directed modulation ✓Internal reasoning ✓Flexible generalization ✓Selectivity ✓
Limited capacity ✓ (k≈25)Ignition-style nonlinear entry ✗ not yet shownPhenomenal consciousness — out of scope

"Flexible generalization" means the same J vector can serve as an argument to many different downstream operations — like one message on the bus that different services each consume in their own way. The paper itself acknowledges that the architectural correspondence to GWT is loose: capacity limits and selectivity show up, but the full "ignition" mechanism has not been found.

Public commentary centers on three points. First, functional access ≠ phenomenal experience — philosopher Ned Block long ago distinguished access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness, and J-space corresponds at most to the former. Second, some commentators argue that many GWT requirements were relaxed to make the definition fit; strip away that framing and it is a finding about computational organization, not about minds. Third, the small-model replication failure mentioned above suggests the scope of the result needs more independent testing. The paper does not claim machine consciousness, but its wording leaves room for imagination.

Code example
import numpy as np
# compare two ways of "entering the workspace": linear/gradual vs ignition (all-or-none)
def linear_access(s):
    return s / s.sum()                     # share the bus in proportion to strength
def ignition_access(s, k=3, theta=0.6, gain=8):
    top = np.argsort(s)[-k:]                 # capacity limit: only the top k compete
    out = np.zeros_like(s)
    act = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-gain * (s[top] - theta)))  # steep sigmoid ≈ threshold
    out[top] = np.where(act > 0.5, 1.0, 0.0)    # above threshold → full broadcast, else silence
    return out
s = np.array([0.2, 0.55, 0.65, 0.9, 0.1, 0.58])  # activation strengths of 6 candidate concepts
print("linear:", linear_access(s).round(2))
print("ignition:", ignition_access(s))   # 0.58 and 0.65 are close, yet one ends at 0, the other at 1
Common misconception + practical scenario
Misconception: "Anthropic proved Claude is conscious." What the paper shows is that there exists a representation satisfying several functional workspace properties. Jumping from "functionally similar" to "has experience" means crossing the hardest problem in consciousness research — which this paper does not, and cannot, do.
📌 BigCat scenario: great material for interdisciplinary thinking — Buddhism layers "mental factors" and "consciousness", neuroscience separates access from phenomenal; now a machine offers an operable, intervenable sample of "access". For the first time, the question "which theories of consciousness are testable?" has a non-biological test bench.
Takeaway + reflection
💡 J-space gives GWT's "access" part an intervenable machine sample — but it lacks "ignition" and says nothing about "experience"; use the analogy with restraint.
🤔 If ignition-style nonlinear entry were someday observed in a model, would that make you more inclined to think it is "conscious"? What would still be missing?

Deeper Reflections

The J-lens and SAEs both claim to read "what the model is thinking". Can they give contradictory answers?
Yes — and the contradiction is itself informative. SAEs start from reconstructing activations and ask "which separable features are stored here?"; they find many features the model never voices (syntax, position, style). The J-lens starts from output influence and only asks "which words does this push toward?". A feature that is prominent in an SAE but invisible to the J-lens is local computational state; conversely, something the J-lens reads but no single SAE feature captures suggests verbalizable concepts may be combinations of features. It's the database difference between "what's in the table" and "what the query returns".
What does "low variance yet behavior-determining" mean for AI safety?
Many monitoring and compression methods assume high-variance directions matter most — PCA reduction, pruning by activation magnitude, filtering features by norm. If the critical, verbalizable reasoning lives in a low-variance subspace, these methods will systematically miss what most needs watching. The flip side is good news: the space to monitor is small and capacity-limited, which in principle is more tractable than monitoring the whole residual stream. Safety design should allocate attention by "causal influence on output", not "numerical size".
What happens if the model knows its J-space is being read?
This is the white-box version of Day 55's obfuscation problem. As soon as the training signal includes J-lens readouts (e.g. penalizing dangerous concepts), optimization pressure may push the relevant computation outside J-space — completing the same reasoning in an "unsayable" form, perhaps at a cost in capability, since the paper shows flexible reasoning depends on J-space. There's a subtle game here: moving reasoning out of the workspace may make it less flexible. Using readout tools only for evaluation, never as a training target, is currently the safest stance.
If human consciousness is also just a "capacity-limited broadcast bus", what does J-space tell us about ourselves?
GWT is a functionalist theory: it says access consciousness is a certain way of organizing information. If you accept that, the emergence of J-space suggests this organization may be a convergent solution for "any system that needs to reuse information flexibly" rather than something uniquely biological — like eyes evolving independently in different species. But if you think the core of consciousness is experience itself (phenomenal consciousness), J-space shows precisely that the function can exist in full without answering the question of experience. The Buddhist distinction between consciousness and mental factors may hint that these two layers should not be conflated.
